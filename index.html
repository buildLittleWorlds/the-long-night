The Long Night

For forty years, Miriam had helped people.

She had carried food up staircases, driven through snow, and sat beside beds long after she wanted to go home. Sometimes she enjoyed the work. Sometimes she continued only because someone needed her.

Then the world changed.

Whenever anyone sincerely wished for another person to be fed, sheltered, or healed, it happened.

There was no special technique. People did not have to concentrate, struggle, or become unusually good. Ordinary kindness was enough. Pretending to care accomplished nothing, but a genuine wish worked immediately.

The wish cost no money, energy, or time that could have been spent on something else. No one elsewhere had to do the work. Nothing was taken from anyone. There was no hidden price.

One morning, Miriam’s granddaughter, Anna, thought of the people Miriam had spent her life helping. Then she thought of people in other cities, and people in places she had never heard of.

Before breakfast, she had relieved as much suffering as Miriam had relieved in forty years.

Miriam knew this was wonderful. She would not have returned a single person to hunger or sickness to make her own life seem more important.

Nevertheless, something troubled her.

“Was what you did this morning as generous as what I did?” she asked.

“I don’t know,” Anna said. “The people were helped just as much.”

“That isn’t quite what I asked.”

“I know.”

They sat quietly for a while.

Then Anna asked, “Has helping become worth less, or does it just cost less?”

Miriam had no answer.

People were not all alike in the new world. Some wished only for their families to be well. Others cared about strangers as readily as friends. Some remained indifferent, although helping would have cost them nothing.

Anna thought these differences still mattered.

“You can still ask who someone cares about,” she told Miriam.

“Yes,” Miriam said. “But you can no longer find out whether they would stay when staying was hard.”

“Perhaps they would.”

“Perhaps. But there used to be people who actually did.”

Miriam began meeting with others who remembered the old world. None of them wanted suffering back. What they missed was the courage, endurance, and self-denial that caring had sometimes required.

They decided to hold a vigil.

For one night each year, they would remain awake in the square outside the old hospital. Some would walk there from distant towns. Some would go without food. They would remember people who had once needed help and people who had given their lives to providing it.

Nobody claimed the vigil would feed, shelter, or heal anyone. Those things could already be accomplished without it. Its purpose was to make concern costly again.

When Miriam told Anna, Anna asked, “Why must you be tired for your concern to count?”

“It already counts.”

“Then what will staying awake add?”

“I don’t know exactly,” Miriam said. “But when someone mattered to me, I used to give them something I could have kept for myself. A night’s sleep. A comfortable afternoon. Sometimes years. I don’t think all of that was merely an unfortunate expense.”

“But giving up sleep won’t give anyone anything now.”

“Not everything we give has to be useful.”

On the evening of the vigil, Anna walked with her grandmother to the square.

Some people had brought the names of those they had cared for. Others had come with no names at all. They stood together as the windows of the old hospital grew dark.

Anna understood why Miriam wanted to be there. She could also imagine children growing up to believe that those who stayed awake longest cared most.

“Do you think someone who goes home is less generous?” she asked.

“No,” Miriam said.

“Do you think staying makes you more generous?”

Miriam looked at the people around her.

“I hope it means something. That isn’t quite the same thing.”

Anna stayed beside her for an hour. Then she kissed her grandmother and went home to bed.

Neither loved anyone less when they parted.

By morning, Miriam would be exhausted. No one would be better fed, better sheltered, or less ill because she had remained.

Anna lay awake for a little while, wondering whether her grandmother was preserving something precious or teaching people to mistake the price of kindness for kindness itself.

A related thought experiment

The Pianist Who Arrived Overnight

Two pianists perform equally magnificent concerts.

One spent twenty years learning. The other received a procedure yesterday that installed exactly the same musical capacities. The procedure supplied understanding, sensitivity, judgment, and the ability to improvise—not merely the ability to reproduce finger movements. Neither pianist is deceiving the audience.

Their performances are equally moving. They can answer the same questions, teach equally well, and respond equally intelligently to unfamiliar music.

Should they receive equal applause, equal payment, and equal respect? Do those three questions have the same answer?

Now imagine that the second pianist feels ashamed. To make the achievement legitimate, she proposes spending the next twenty years performing the exercises the first pianist completed. These exercises will add nothing to her already complete abilities.

Would doing so earn something she presently lacks? Can effort acquire value when it comes after the achievement it supposedly justifies?

A real-world debate · September 2026

Keeping the Struggle in the Loop?

AI is now entering research mathematics, producing proofs for major problems and raising the prospect of obtaining results far faster than people could discover them unaided. A correct result, however, is not the same thing as a person understanding it. The speed of production has sharpened a question familiar from the story: what happens when we can obtain the result without undergoing the experience that once produced it?

On September 11, 2026, a declaration initially signed by 25 Fields medalists, “A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics,” warned that a race to generate solutions could undermine mathematical understanding. Working on problems also develops judgment, new questions, and a community able to explain and extend the results. Read through the lens of The Long Night, this is a plea to keep people engaged in the struggle because that engagement helps make mathematicians, not just mathematics. The declaration also welcomes AI that accelerates understanding; it does not demand difficulty simply for its own sake.

Fields medalist Timothy Gowers offered a skeptical counterpoint in his September 17 response, “Why I didn’t sign the Fields medallists’ letter.” He acknowledges the deep understanding and sense of ownership that can come from solving a problem oneself. But he questions whether losing that particular experience must mean less mathematical progress. AI might help people actively understand more mathematics, including through carefully chosen hints. In the terms of our thought experiments, his challenge is that struggle needs a justification beyond the fact that it used to be necessary. This is an interpretation of his argument, not a claim that he dismisses demanding work or the human mathematical community.

The difference from the overnight pianist matters: an AI-generated proof does not automatically install understanding in its reader. But if AI could help people acquire the same understanding with less struggle, would insisting on the old difficulty preserve something precious—or mistake the price of understanding for understanding itself?
